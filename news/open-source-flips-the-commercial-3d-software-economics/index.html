Vendor lock-in is now the top reason studios pick open tools

Clients increasingly demand ownership of their pipelines. That changes how studios pitch and what they budget for.

11 September 2026 / Vision For Xperiences Studio Desk / 3 min read
open sourceblenderproduction infrastructuresoftware economicsvendor lock-in

Fifty-five percent of organizations now cite vendor lock-in as the primary reason they choose open source over commercial software, up 68 year-over-year. Buried in 2026 industry reports, that number matters more than another Blender feature drop because it describes what clients are actually asking for in briefs: can we own this pipeline outright? Will a five-year project still be portable if a vendor raises prices or discontinues a product line?

The economics have flipped. Ten years ago, open source meant good enough for hobbyists. Now it means no recurring license spend, no permission gates, no hostage situation. Blender does modeling, sculpting, rigging, animation, simulation, rendering, and compositing in one package. FreeCAD handles parametric CAD work with support for open formats like STEP and IGES. Godot ships with a complete 3D engine. The feature parity with expensive alternatives keeps widening, and the permission structure keeps mattering more.

The conversation has actually changed

A producer budgeting a project used to ask: do we have Maya seats? Now they ask: do we need them? Vision For Xperiences works across budget ranges, from small motion graphics gigs that don't justify annual subscriptions up through mid-size installation projects. That positioning makes open source infrastructure a practical choice, not a compromise. When a client asks whether they can export project files in a format they control, the answer is simpler if you've been working in Blender from the start.

The shift is not "open source is replacing commercial software." It's that per-seat subscriptions, feature tiers, and lock-in on proprietary formats are now actively unpopular with buyers. Studios that treat agility as a selling point are the ones taking this seriously.

What it changes on the quote

A shot that would have meant eight months of Adobe subscription fees now means project files a client can hand to a different vendor next year if they choose. Below EUR 5,000, software licensing becomes a line item that moves the economics unfavorably. Above that, it's still visible on a spreadsheet. Teams comfortable with Blender's workflow can price more competitively because the cost structure is different.

Vision For Xperiences publishes budget bands on the brief form, starting under EUR 1,000, and that's where open source actually changes the math. A small consultancy or agency that needs 3D product visuals for two weeks does not need a six-month Substance Painter license. They need the files back.

What is still hard

Vendor lock-in as a driver doesn't mean cost disappears. It means cost is now accompanied by portability. For VFX specifically, that's a real challenge: proprietary looks, proprietary plugins, proprietary color spaces, but it's the question more clients are asking. Blender's ecosystem of community-built plugins handles a lot of it. The infrastructure shift is slower than the software adoption because teams are still learning how to build pipelines around open source tools that actually hold up.

For interactive installations or live visuals work where you're building a one-time show, this matters less. For product animation or VFX work that lives in a client's archive for years, it matters increasingly. That's why we treat open tools not as a backup plan but as a real option when scoping the first conversation with a client.

Quick answers

Does Blender really replace Maya for professional VFX?

Blender handles the core VFX pipeline—compositing, tracking, simulation—at a professional level. The gap is in specialized departments and legacy pipelines. A team comfortable with Blender's workflow can do broadcast-quality work. The constraint is usually team expertise, not software capability.

If we switch to open source tools, will we be at a disadvantage?

No, but it requires reskilling. Blender and FreeCAD are genuinely capable. The advantage is cost and portability. The risk is timeline if your team is deeply embedded in another tool's UI.

What do clients actually mean by vendor lock-in?

They mean they want deliverables in open formats they can read in 10 years without paying subscription fees. They also want the option to move to a different vendor without restarting work. Proprietary file formats lock them out of both.
