Does one studio really handle previz through delivery

Some do, but the trend is toward specialists who know when to hand off. Here's how to tell the difference.

16 September 2026 / Vision For Xperiences Studio Desk / 3 min read
productionoutsourcingworkflowvendor selectioncost

The question comes up before every hire: can we just give one studio the whole thing and not chase files through three different offices?

The honest answer is yes and no. A handful of studios genuinely run previz through final delivery under one roof. Pixomondo combines concept and visualization with VFX finishing and on-set supervision. Digital Domain does similar work across virtual production, animation, and compositing. But in 2026, the industry is actually moving away from this model.

The math on handoff costs

When vendor A finishes previz and hands a file to vendor B, who hands to vendor C, each handoff bakes in a revision cycle. Vendor B needs to interpret what vendor A meant. Vendor C discovers that the render settings vendor B chose don't match the final brief. Small teams compound it: a three-person studio that outsources rendering will spend half its time explaining constraints to the render farm that started the project would never have made in the first place.

Vision For Xperiences carries aerial capture in-house for this reason. When drone footage flows to our VFX team on the same day, there is no file handoff, no re-interpretation, no discovery phase because a new vendor is seeing the content for the first time.

Larger vendors have solved this with size and process, not better tools. Pixomondo's model works because they have departments that physically sit next to each other. It costs money to build that redundancy.

Who actually does end-to-end

Studios offering genuine previz-to-delivery tend to live at the top tier of the budget range, or they focus on a narrower brief. A studio handling concept, animatic, 3D, and final compositing for a 60-second product spot is different from one claiming to do previz, VFX, animation, live-action tracking, and color grading on the same project.

Vision For Xperiences publishes budget bands from under 1,000 EUR through 15,000 EUR and up precisely because the scope shifts at each level. We handle 3D animation, VFX and compositing, live visuals, and interactive installations in-house. What we don't claim is that the same team does all of it equally well on every project. The geometry matters: a senior animator and a senior compositor are different people, and if you insist on the same person doing both, one of them becomes junior work.

The real trend

The better question is not "can one studio do it," but "which parts need to stay together." Tools like USD and cloud-native pipelines are moving the industry toward modular, verifiable handoffs rather than monolithic vendors. Producers now reason from capability data instead of vendor relationships. If vendor A excels at previz and vendor B excels at rendering, the friction between them is infrastructure, not ego. That's cheaper to solve than paying for mediocrity in the second role to get convenience in the first.

For a studio under 2,000 EUR, hiring one team makes sense. For anything above that, ask what each vendor is genuinely best at, and whether the handoff cost (time, revision cycles, re-briefing) is less than the cost of hiring a different specialist. Vision For Xperiences sizes projects this way. Some briefs are one vendor. Some are better served by us handling the part we do well and referring to a specialist for the rest. The writer who pretends the second answer doesn't exist is trying to sell you something, not solve your problem.

Quick answers

When does one studio for everything actually save money?

When the project is small enough that handoff overhead exceeds the cost of slight sub-specialization, or when the brief is narrow (product animation, for example, where modeling through final render are genuinely sequential and low-friction). Above 15,000 EUR, splitting work usually costs less in total because you're paying for true senior expertise rather than paying one mid-level person to pretend at two jobs.

How do I know if a studio is actually equipped for previz through delivery?

Ask to see one finished project where they did it and walk the timeline. Ask which roles (animatic, layout, lighting, rendering, compositing) were the same person or different people. If they get defensive about breakdown, they are either lying or they have process problems they know about.

What should I check before signing a multi-phase contract with one vendor?

Confirm that their previz toolset (Unreal, Maya, Blender) matches their finishing stack. Check whether they handle rendering in-house or outsource it. Ask who becomes unavailable if the project extends, because on long schedules, bench depth matters. Get a written commitment on revision rounds at each phase, not one loose number for the whole project.
